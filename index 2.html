Phasors and Three-Phase

Everything in AC circuit analysis follows from one move: stop solving differential equations in time, and start doing arithmetic with rotating arrows. This page builds that move from scratch, then rides it all the way to three-phase power measurement.

Two chapters, one idea. Read this once now; it will make more sense the second time.

A resistor obeys \(v = Ri\). A capacitor obeys \(i = C\,dv/dt\). An inductor obeys \(v = L\,di/dt\). Mix them and Kirchhoff's laws hand you a differential equation — painful, and you have to re-solve it every time the circuit changes.

But if every source in the circuit is a sinusoid at one single frequency, then every voltage and current in the circuit is also a sinusoid at that same frequency. Only two things can differ: amplitude and phase. So carrying \(\cos(\omega t)\) around in the algebra is carrying information that never changes.

The central move

Strip out \(\omega t\). Represent each sinusoid by a single complex number holding just its magnitude and phase — a phasor. Differentiation becomes multiplication by \(j\omega\). The differential equations collapse into Ohm's law with complex numbers, and you can use every DC technique you already know: series, parallel, KVL, KCL, mesh, node, Thévenin.

Chapter 2 builds that machine and uses it on one-phase circuits, ending with power and power-factor correction. Chapter 3 takes three of those circuits, offsets them by 120°, and shows why the entire world's electricity is generated that way.

PREComplex numbers, only what you need

If this section is comfortable, skim it. If it is not, nothing after §5 will work.

A complex number is a point on a plane. Two ways to name that point:

\[ \underbrace{A + jB}_{\text{rectangular}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{r\angle\theta = re^{j\theta}}_{\text{polar}} \]

with \(j=\sqrt{-1}\) (engineers use \(j\), not \(i\), because \(i\) is already current). Converting:

\[ r=\sqrt{A^2+B^2},\quad \theta=\arctan\!\frac{B}{A} \qquad\qquad A=r\cos\theta,\quad B=r\sin\theta \]
Trap · the arctangent quadrant

\(\arctan(B/A)\) on a calculator only returns \(-90^\circ\) to \(+90^\circ\). For \(-3-j4\) it gives \(53.13^\circ\), which is flatly wrong — the point is in the third quadrant, so the answer is \(-126.87^\circ\). Always sketch the point first. Or set your calculator to complex mode and let it do the conversion.

Which form for which operation

This is the whole practical skill. Adding in polar form, or multiplying in rectangular form, is how people waste time in exams.

\(j = 1\angle 90^\circ\)  ·  \(-j = 1\angle-90^\circ\)  ·  \(1/j = -j\)  ·  \(j\cdot j = -1\)

Multiplying by \(j\) is a +90° rotation. Nothing else. That single fact is why inductors make voltage lead current — you will meet it again in §6.

01Why AC at all

Four reasons, and one of them is the reason.

Roughly 90% of electrical energy is consumed as alternating current. That is not historical accident:

  1. Transformers only work on AC. This is the reason. A transformer needs a changing magnetic flux to induce voltage in the secondary; DC produces constant flux and therefore nothing. Transformers let you change voltage levels cheaply, with no moving parts and ~99% efficiency.
  2. Which makes efficient transmission possible. Line loss is \(I^2R\). Send the same power \(P=VI\) at 10× the voltage and you need 1/10 the current, so you lose 1/100 the power in the wires. Thailand transmits at 500 kV and 230 kV, then steps down to 220/380 V at your building.
  3. Three-phase AC machines are better. Higher efficiency, higher power density, and no commutator or brushes to wear out (Part 2).
  4. Interrupting AC is easier. The current passes through zero 100 times a second at 50 Hz, which gives a circuit breaker a natural moment to extinguish the arc.
Physical anchor

Every wall socket in Thailand is 220 V, 50 Hz. That 220 is not the peak — it is the RMS value, and the peak is \(220\sqrt2 \approx 311\) V. Section 4 explains why we quote it that way, and it is the single most-missed idea in this chapter.

Trap · mixing units inside one bracket

You will constantly write things like \(\sin(1000t + 60^\circ)\), where \(1000t\) is in radians and \(60^\circ\) is in degrees. Every textbook does it and it is technically an abuse of notation. It is fine — just never let your calculator evaluate the whole bracket in one mode. Handle the \(\omega t\) part and the \(\theta\) part separately.

The rotating-vector picture

Take an arrow of length \(A\) and spin it counter-clockwise at \(\omega\) rad/s starting from angle \(\theta\). Project its tip onto the vertical axis at every instant, and you trace out \(A\sin(\omega t+\theta)\). Project onto the horizontal axis instead and you get \(A\cos(\omega t+\theta)\).

That picture is the phasor, three sections early. Play with it now so that §5 is a formality rather than a shock.

03Lead, lag, and the cosine rule

Comparing two sinusoids has exactly four steps. Skip one and the answer is wrong by 90° or 180° — the classic silent error.

The four-step comparison procedure (หลักการเปรียบเทียบมุมต่างเฟส)
  1. Same frequency. If \(\omega_1\ne\omega_2\), the phase difference changes with time and "lead/lag" is meaningless. Stop.
  2. Same function. Convert both to sine, or both to cosine. Never compare a sine against a cosine directly.
  3. Same sign. Both amplitudes must be positive. A leading minus sign is a 180° phase shift in disguise.
  4. Subtract the angles. \(\angle x_1 - \angle x_2 > 0\) means \(x_1\) leads \(x_2\).

The conversion identities

You only need these four. The two on the right are the ones that catch people.

\[ \cos\theta = \sin(\theta + 90^\circ) \qquad \sin\theta=\cos(\theta-90^\circ) \] \[ -\sin\theta = \sin(\theta \pm 180^\circ) \qquad -\cos\theta = \cos(\theta \pm 180^\circ) \]
A memory hook

Cosine peaks before sine — cosine is at its maximum when \(t=0\), sine is still climbing. So cosine leads sine by 90°. If you remember only that, you can rebuild every identity above by sketching.

04Average and RMS

Why the socket is labelled 220 V when it actually swings between −311 V and +311 V.

Average value: useless for sinusoids

\[ X_{avg}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} x(t)\,dt \]

For any pure sinusoid this is exactly zero, no matter the amplitude, frequency or phase — the positive and negative halves cancel. So the average tells you nothing about how "big" an AC signal is. It is still useful for rectified waveforms, which is why it appears in your notes.

RMS: the definition that actually means something

The definition to memorise (ค่าประสิทธิผล)

The RMS (or effective) value of an AC waveform is the DC voltage that would dissipate the same average power in the same resistor.

Derive it in three lines. Average power in \(R\):

\[ P_{avg}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}\frac{v^2(t)}{R}\,dt \qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{DC}=\frac{V_{DC}^2}{R} \]

Set them equal and solve for \(V_{DC}\):

\[ \boxed{\;V_{rms}=V_{eff}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}v^2(t)\,dt}\;} \]

Read the name backwards, right to left: take the Root of the Mean of the Square. Squaring is what removes the sign problem — that is the whole idea.

For a sinusoid the integral evaluates to a clean result:

\[ V_{rms}=\frac{V_p}{\sqrt2}=0.707\,V_p \qquad I_{rms}=\frac{I_p}{\sqrt2} \]
Trap · \(1/\sqrt2\) is only for sinusoids

A square wave of ±10 V has \(V_{rms}=10\) V, not 7.07. A triangle wave has \(V_p/\sqrt3\). Half-wave rectified sine has \(V_p/2\). Always go back to the integral definition unless you are certain the waveform is a pure sine.

Self-check · retrieval practice

A Thai wall socket is labelled 220 V, 50 Hz. What is the peak-to-peak voltage?

622 V. The 220 is RMS, so the peak is \(220\sqrt2=311\) V, and peak-to-peak is twice that: 622 V. This is why insulation on 220 V equipment is rated well above 220 V — it has to hold off 311 V every 10 ms.

05Phasors

The one idea the rest of the course rests on.

Start from Euler's relation, \(e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta\). Any cosine can be written as the real part of a rotating complex exponential:

\[ i(t)=I_p\cos(\omega t+\theta) = \mathrm{Re}\big\{I_p e^{j(\omega t+\theta)}\big\} = \mathrm{Re}\Big\{\underbrace{I_p e^{j\theta}}_{\text{fixed}}\cdot \underbrace{e^{j\omega t}}_{\text{spins}}\Big\} \]

The second factor is the same for every signal in the circuit. So carry only the first, converted to RMS magnitude:

Phasor definition used in this course \[ i(t)=I_p\cos(\omega t+\theta_I) \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{I}=\frac{I_p}{\sqrt2}\angle\theta_I = I_{rms}\angle\theta_I \]

Two non-negotiable conventions in 2102391: convert to cosine first, and the phasor magnitude is the RMS value, not the peak.

Trap · the \(\sqrt2\) that costs marks

Some textbooks (and many YouTube videos) define phasors with peak magnitude. Both conventions are self-consistent, but you cannot mix them, and the power formulas change: with RMS phasors \(S=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{I}^{*}\); with peak phasors \(S=\tfrac12\mathbf{V}\mathbf{I}^{*}\). This course uses RMS. When you go back to \(i(t)\) at the end, remember to multiply by \(\sqrt2\).

Two rules and two limits

  • Only voltages and currents are phasors. Impedance \(Z\) and complex power \(S\) are complex numbers, but they are not phasors — they do not correspond to a rotating sinusoid.
  • Only one frequency at a time. If a circuit has a 50 Hz source and a 150 Hz source, you must solve it twice and superpose in the time domain.

The lesson

Two sinusoids at the same frequency add to a third sinusoid at that same frequency. Phasors turn that fact into vector addition. No calculus, no identities.

\(v(t) = 50\sin(377t + 20^\circ)\) V. What is its phasor in this course's convention?

\(35.36\angle-70^\circ\) V. Two operations, both required. Convert sine to cosine: \(50\sin(377t+20^\circ)=50\cos(377t+20^\circ-90^\circ)=50\cos(377t-70^\circ)\). Then divide the peak by \(\sqrt2\): \(50/\sqrt2=35.36\). Missing the \(-90^\circ\) is the most common single error in this chapter.

06R, L, C and impedance

Where the calculus disappears and Ohm's law comes back.

Resistor — nothing happens

\(v=Ri\) holds instant by instant, so in phasor form \(\mathbf{V}=R\,\mathbf{I}\). Voltage and current are in phase.

Inductor — voltage leads by 90°

Faraday: \(v=L\,di/dt\). Put \(i(t)=I_p\cos(\omega t+\theta_I)\) in:

\[ v(t)=-\omega L I_p\sin(\omega t+\theta_I)=\omega L I_p\cos(\omega t+\theta_I+90^\circ) \]

Amplitude scaled by \(\omega L\), phase advanced by 90°. Multiplying a phasor by \(j\) does exactly that, so:

\[ \boxed{\;\mathbf{V}=j\omega L\,\mathbf{I}\;}\qquad Z_L = j\omega L = \omega L\angle 90^\circ \]

Capacitor — voltage lags by 90°

\(i=C\,dv/dt\). Same argument, run the other way:

\[ \boxed{\;\mathbf{V}=\frac{1}{j\omega C}\,\mathbf{I}\;}\qquad Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C}=\frac{1}{\omega C}\angle-90^\circ = -\,\frac{j}{\omega C} \]
The mnemonic everyone uses: ELI the ICE man

ELI — in an inductor (L), voltage (E) comes before current (I).
ICE — in a capacitor (C), current (I) comes before voltage (E).

Physical reason, not just a mnemonic: an inductor resists a change in current, so current lags behind the voltage pushing it. A capacitor must be charged before its voltage rises, so the charging current arrives first.

At DC an inductor is just a coil of wire — a short. A capacitor is two plates with a gap — an open. Set \(\omega=0\) in the formulas and you get exactly that. If a result ever looks wrong, check the DC limit.

Trap · impedances in series add, magnitudes do not

\(Z_{eq}=Z_1+Z_2\) is complex addition. If \(Z_1=100\ \Omega\) (resistor) and \(Z_2=j100\ \Omega\) (inductor), then \(Z_{eq}=100+j100\) which has magnitude \(141.4\ \Omega\), not 200 Ω. Adding \(|Z_1|+|Z_2|\) is one of the most common errors on this material.

Reactance \(X\) is the imaginary part of \(Z\): \(X_L=+\omega L\), \(X_C=-1/(\omega C)\). Note the sign convention — capacitive reactance enters \(Z=R+jX\) as a negative \(X\). Your homework sheets often quote \(X_C=50\ \Omega\) as a positive magnitude and expect you to insert it as \(-j50\).

Four steps. Every single-phase problem in this course is these four steps.

  1. Convert every source to a phasor (cosine first, then ÷ √2).

08Power: P, Q and S

Three different "powers", three different units, and only one of them is energy you actually consume.

Start from the definition. With \(v(t)=V_p\cos\omega t\) and \(i(t)=I_p\cos(\omega t-\theta)\), instantaneous power is

\[ p(t)=v(t)i(t)=\underbrace{V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta\,(1+\cos2\omega t)}_{\text{never negative — real transfer}}+\underbrace{V_{rms}I_{rms}\sin\theta\,\sin2\omega t}_{\text{averages to zero — sloshing}} \]

Two structurally different terms. Read them:

  • The first term has average \(V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta\) and never goes negative. This is energy leaving the source permanently.
  • The second term has average zero. It is energy flowing out to the reactive elements during part of the cycle and flowing straight back during the rest. Nothing is consumed — but it still occupies the wires.

Trap · the conjugate is not optional

\(S=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{I}^{*}\), never \(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{I}\). Without the conjugate you get an angle of \(\theta_V+\theta_I\) instead of \(\theta_V-\theta_I\), and both \(P\) and \(Q\) come out wrong. The conjugate is what makes the angle of \(S\) equal the angle of \(Z\).

Why Q is positive for inductors

For an inductor \(\theta_V-\theta_I=+90^\circ\), so \(\sin\theta=+1\) and \(Q=+VI\). For a capacitor \(\theta_V-\theta_I=-90^\circ\), so \(Q=-VI\). Convention:

  • Inductors consume reactive power — \(Q>0\)
  • Capacitors supply reactive power — \(Q<0\)

That opposition is the entire basis of power factor correction (§10): install capacitors to cancel the inductors' Q.

Shortcuts worth memorising

\[ S_R = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R} \qquad S_L = jI^2\omega L = j\frac{V^2}{\omega L} \qquad S_C = -\frac{jI^2}{\omega C} = -jV^2\omega C \]

(All voltages and currents RMS.) These let you skip the phasor arithmetic when you already know the current through, or voltage across, an element.

Conservation

Complex power is conserved: \(S_{source}=\sum S_{elements}\), which means \(P\) and \(Q\) are separately conserved. Apparent power \(|S|\) is not — you cannot add VA magnitudes. This is the fastest way to check any power calculation.

09Power factor

One number that tells you how much of the current in your wires is doing useful work.

\[ \text{PF}=\frac{P}{|S|}=\cos(\theta_V-\theta_I)=\cos\theta \]

Because \(S=P+jQ\) forms a right triangle with \(|S|\) as the hypotenuse, and because the angle of \(S\) equals the angle of \(Z\), all three of these triangles are similar:

\[ \frac{Q}{P}=\tan\theta \qquad\qquad \frac{X}{R}=\tan\theta \qquad\qquad \theta_S=\theta_Z=\theta_V-\theta_I \]
Lagging or leading — say which, always

PF is a cosine, so \(\cos(+30^\circ)=\cos(-30^\circ)=0.866\). The number alone is ambiguous. You must state:

  • Lagging — current lags voltage, \(\theta>0\), \(Q>0\), inductive. Motors, transformers, fluorescent ballasts. This is the overwhelmingly common case.
  • Leading — current leads voltage, \(\theta<0\), \(Q<0\), capacitive. Capacitor banks, some long lightly-loaded cables, over-excited synchronous machines.

10Power factor correction

Bolt capacitors across the load until the reactive power mostly cancels. Four lines of arithmetic, and it is on every exam.

The governing assumption

Capacitors consume no real power. So \(P\) is unchanged by correction:

\[ P_{new}=P_{old} \]

Everything else follows from holding \(P\) fixed while shrinking \(Q\).

The procedure

  1. From the original PF, get \(\theta_1=\cos^{-1}(\text{PF}_1)\) and \(Q_1=P\tan\theta_1\).
  2. From the target PF, get \(\theta_2=\cos^{-1}(\text{PF}_2)\) and \(Q_2=P\tan\theta_2\).
  3. The capacitor must supply the difference: \[ \boxed{\;Q_C=Q_1-Q_2=P\big(\tan\theta_1-\tan\theta_2\big)\;} \]
  4. Size the capacitor from \(Q_C = V^2\omega C\): \[ C=\frac{Q_C}{V^2\omega}=\frac{Q_C}{2\pi f V^2} \qquad\text{or}\qquad X_C=\frac{V^2}{Q_C} \]

11Why three phases

Two reasons that are worth real money, and one that is worth a lot of copper.

Take three identical single-phase sources, same amplitude and frequency, but shifted 120° from each other:

\[ v_A=\sqrt2\,V\cos\omega t,\quad v_B=\sqrt2\,V\cos(\omega t-120^\circ),\quad v_C=\sqrt2\,V\cos(\omega t-240^\circ) \]

1 · Constant total power

In a single-phase circuit, \(p(t)\) pulses at \(2\omega\) — twice per cycle it passes through zero. A single-phase motor therefore delivers pulsating torque and vibrates. Add the three instantaneous powers of a balanced three-phase system and the pulsations cancel exactly:

\[ p_A(t)+p_B(t)+p_C(t) = 3VI\cos\theta = \text{constant} \]

Smooth torque, no vibration, self-starting motors. Panel 6 below shows this directly — watch the black total-power trace stay flat while the individual traces pulse.

2 · The neutral carries nothing

Three separate single-phase circuits need six wires. Tie the three return conductors together into one neutral and you need four. But for a balanced load:

\[ \mathbf{I}_N=\mathbf{I}_A+\mathbf{I}_B+\mathbf{I}_C = I\angle-\theta + I\angle(-120^\circ-\theta)+I\angle(-240^\circ-\theta)= 0 \]

Three equal vectors at 120° apart always sum to zero. So the neutral carries no current at all — you can shrink it, or in transmission omit it entirely and run three wires.

Try it on your calculator right now

Add \(10\angle0^\circ + 10\angle-120^\circ + 10\angle-240^\circ\). You should get exactly 0. If you do not, your calculator is in radian mode.

3 · Less copper for the same power

Combining the two effects above, delivering a given amount of power three-phase needs substantially less conductor material than single-phase — which is why transmission and distribution are three-phase worldwide.

13Wye (Y) connection

Three sources or loads sharing one common point. The √3 appears here.

Vocabulary — get this straight before anything else
  • Phase voltage \(V_p\) (แรงดันเฟส): across one element. Written \(V_{AN},V_{BN},V_{CN}\) — note the N.
  • Line voltage \(V_L\) (แรงดันสาย): between two lines. Written \(V_{AB},V_{BC},V_{CA}\) — two letters, no N.
  • Phase current \(I_p\): through one element.
  • Line current \(I_L\): in the wire feeding the load.

Deriving \(V_L=\sqrt3\,V_p\)

Line voltage is a difference of two phase voltages, by KVL:

\[ \mathbf{V}_{AB}=\mathbf{V}_{AN}-\mathbf{V}_{BN} \]

With abc sequence, \(\mathbf{V}_{AN}=V\angle0^\circ\) and \(\mathbf{V}_{BN}=V\angle-120^\circ\). Note \(-\mathbf{V}_{BN}=V\angle60^\circ\), so

\[ \mathbf{V}_{AB}=V\angle0^\circ+V\angle60^\circ = V + V(\cos60^\circ + j\sin60^\circ) \] \[ = V\left(1+\tfrac12\right)+jV\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \tfrac{3}{2}V + j\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}V \] \[ |\mathbf{V}_{AB}|=V\sqrt{\tfrac94+\tfrac34}=V\sqrt3,\qquad \angle\mathbf{V}_{AB}=\arctan\frac{\sqrt3/2}{3/2}=30^\circ \]

15The 30°+θ master rule

One relationship that holds for both connections. If you memorise one thing from Chapter 3, memorise this.

Master rule (abc sequence)

The line voltage leads the line current by \(30^\circ + \theta\), where \(\theta\) is the load impedance angle.

True for a Y load. True for a Δ load. True for any mix of balanced loads in parallel. It is what lets you work with line quantities directly and never draw the internal connection at all.

16Three-phase power

One formula in line quantities, and it works for both connections.

Per phase, exactly as in Part 1:

\[ P_{1\phi}=V_p I_p\cos\theta \]

Three identical phases:

\[ P_{3\phi}=3V_p I_p\cos\theta \]

Now substitute. In Y, \(V_p = V_L/\sqrt3\) and \(I_p=I_L\). In Δ, \(V_p=V_L\) and \(I_p=I_L/\sqrt3\). Either way the \(3/\sqrt3=\sqrt3\):

The formula you will use constantly \[ P_{3\phi}=\sqrt3\,V_L I_L\cos\theta \qquad Q_{3\phi}=\sqrt3\,V_L I_L\sin\theta \qquad |S_{3\phi}|=\sqrt3\,V_L I_L \]

All in line quantities, valid for Y and Δ alike.

Trap · the √3 is not a power factor

\(\theta\) in \(\cos\theta\) is still the load angle — the angle between phase voltage and phase current — not the \(30^\circ+\theta\) between line quantities. The \(\sqrt3\) already absorbed the 30°. Writing \(P=\sqrt3 V_L I_L\cos(30^\circ+\theta)\) is wrong and is a favourite exam distractor.

17Y ↔ Δ conversion

Convert one to the other so you can use the formula you actually remember.

General (unbalanced) case, Δ → Y:

\[ Z_{ao}=\frac{Z_{ab}Z_{ca}}{Z_{ab}+Z_{bc}+Z_{ca}},\quad Z_{bo}=\frac{Z_{bc}Z_{ab}}{Z_{ab}+Z_{bc}+Z_{ca}},\quad Z_{co}=\frac{Z_{ca}Z_{bc}}{Z_{ab}+Z_{bc}+Z_{ca}} \]

18Balanced loads in parallel

A motor and a heater bank on the same busbar. Add complex powers, never currents.

Method
  1. Reduce each load to a three-phase complex power \(S_k = P_k + jQ_k\).
  2. Add them: \(S_T=\sum S_k\). (This is why complex power exists.)
  3. Total line current from \(|S_T| = \sqrt3 V_L I_L\).
  4. Overall PF from \(P_T/|S_T|\).

You can add the line-current phasors instead, and you will get the same answer, but you must track each one's angle relative to a common reference. Adding complex powers is faster and less error-prone.

19Measuring three-phase power

A wattmeter reads \(\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{V}_{PC}\mathbf{I}_{CC}^{*})\). Everything in this section follows from that one line.

A wattmeter has two coils: a current coil (CC) in series with the line, and a potential coil (PC) across a voltage. It displays

\[ W=\mathrm{Re}\big(\mathbf{V}_{PC}\,\mathbf{I}_{CC}^{*}\big)=V_{PC}\,I_{CC}\cos\!\big(\angle\mathbf{V}_{PC}-\angle\mathbf{I}_{CC}\big) \]

That is all a wattmeter does. Every result below is just that formula with different wiring.

Three-wattmeter method

One meter per phase, each PC referenced to neutral:

\[ P_{3\phi}=W_A+W_B+W_C=\mathrm{Re}\big(\mathbf{V}_{AN}\mathbf{I}_A^{*}+\mathbf{V}_{BN}\mathbf{I}_B^{*}+\mathbf{V}_{CN}\mathbf{I}_C^{*}\big) \]

Works for anything — balanced or unbalanced. But it needs access to the neutral, so it only applies to 4-wire systems.

Two-wattmeter method

Here is the elegant part. Put the current coils in lines A and B, and reference both potential coils to line C. Then \(W_C\) would read \(\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{V}_{CC}\mathbf{I}_C^{*})=0\) because \(\mathbf{V}_{CC}=0\) — so the third meter is redundant. Proof:

\[ P_{3\phi}=\mathrm{Re}\big(\mathbf{V}_{AN}\mathbf{I}_A^{*}+\mathbf{V}_{BN}\mathbf{I}_B^{*}+\mathbf{V}_{CN}\mathbf{I}_C^{*}\big) \]

In a 3-wire system \(\mathbf{I}_A+\mathbf{I}_B+\mathbf{I}_C=0\), so \(\mathbf{I}_C^{*}=-(\mathbf{I}_A^{*}+\mathbf{I}_B^{*})\). Substitute:

\[ =\mathrm{Re}\big[(\mathbf{V}_{AN}-\mathbf{V}_{CN})\mathbf{I}_A^{*}+(\mathbf{V}_{BN}-\mathbf{V}_{CN})\mathbf{I}_B^{*}\big] = \mathrm{Re}\big(\mathbf{V}_{AC}\mathbf{I}_A^{*}+\mathbf{V}_{BC}\mathbf{I}_B^{*}\big) \]
Two-wattmeter method \[ P_{3\phi}=W_A+W_B \]

Valid for any 3-wire system, balanced or not. Only needs two meters and no neutral. This is how three-phase power is measured in practice.

Power factor straight off the two meters \[ \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\sqrt3\,\frac{W_A-W_B}{W_A+W_B}\right) \]

Two readings, and you know both the power and the power factor without any other instrument.

20Unbalanced loads

When the three impedances differ, the symmetry shortcuts die. Fall back on ordinary circuit analysis.

A load is unbalanced if the three phase impedances are not identical — different \(R+jX\), or the same magnitude but different angle, or different real power per phase. In practice that is most low-voltage buildings, where single-phase loads are distributed unevenly across the three phases.

Case 1 · Y load, four wires (neutral present)

The easy case

The neutral holds each phase voltage fixed at its source value. So the three phases are completely independent — solve each as a separate single-phase circuit:

\[ \mathbf{I}_A=\frac{\mathbf{V}_{AN}}{Z_A},\qquad \mathbf{I}_B=\frac{\mathbf{V}_{BN}}{Z_B},\qquad \mathbf{I}_C=\frac{\mathbf{V}_{CN}}{Z_C} \]

Then the neutral carries whatever is left over: \(\;\mathbf{I}_N=\mathbf{I}_A+\mathbf{I}_B+\mathbf{I}_C\ne0\).

Case 2 · Y load, three wires (no neutral)

Now the load's star point can float away from the source neutral. Two standard methods:

  • Mesh analysis — two loops, driven by the line voltages \(\mathbf{V}_{AB}\) and \(\mathbf{V}_{BC}\).
  • Neutral displacement (Millman) — find the voltage of the floating star point \(n'\) relative to the source neutral \(n\), then treat each phase individually.
\[ \mathbf{V}_{n'n}=\frac{\mathbf{V}_{AN}/Z_A+\mathbf{V}_{BN}/Z_B+\mathbf{V}_{CN}/Z_C}{1/Z_A+1/Z_B+1/Z_C},\qquad \mathbf{I}_A=\frac{\mathbf{V}_{AN}-\mathbf{V}_{n'n}}{Z_A} \]

TRAPSTen ways to lose marks

#The mistakeThe fix

Two checks that catch most errors before you hand in

Conservation. \(\sum P_{elements}=P_{source}\) and \(\sum Q_{elements}=Q_{source}\). If they don't balance, something upstream is wrong.

\(\theta_2=\cos^{-1}0.98 = 11.48^\circ\), \(\tan\theta_2 = 0.2030\) → \(Q_2 = 101.5\) kVAR.

\(Q_C = 339.4\) kVAR.

Current ratio \(= 0.75/0.98 = 0.765\), so the line current drops by 23.5%. The \(I^2R\) losses drop by \(1-0.765^2 = 41.4\%\).

M7
An unbalanced Y load on a 4-wire 220/380 V system has \(Z_A = 22\ \Omega\), \(Z_B = 44\ \Omega\), \(Z_C = 22\angle60^\circ\ \Omega\). Find the neutral current.

Four-wire, so each phase is independent with \(V_p = 220\) V.

\(\mathbf{I}_A = 220\angle0^\circ/22 = 10.00\angle0^\circ = 10.000 + j0\)

\(\mathbf{I}_B = 220\angle-120^\circ/44 = 5.00\angle-120^\circ = -2.500 - j4.330\)

\(\mathbf{I}_C = 220\angle120^\circ/(22\angle60^\circ) = 10.00\angle60^\circ = 5.000 + j8.660\)

\(\mathbf{I}_N = 12.500 + j4.330 = 13.23\angle19.11^\circ\) A. Larger than one of the phase currents — unbalance is not a small effect.

M8
Same three impedances as M7, but connected in Δ across the 380 V lines (abc, \(\mathbf{V}_{AB}=380\angle30^\circ\)). Find \(\mathbf{I}_A\).

Take \(Z_{AB}=22\), \(Z_{BC}=44\), \(Z_{CA}=22\angle60^\circ\ \Omega\). Line voltages: \(\mathbf{V}_{AB}=380\angle30^\circ\), \(\mathbf{V}_{BC}=380\angle-90^\circ\), \(\mathbf{V}_{CA}=380\angle150^\circ\) V.

\(\mathbf{I}_{AB} = 380\angle30^\circ/22 = 17.27\angle30^\circ = 14.957+j8.636\) A

\(\mathbf{I}_{CA} = 380\angle150^\circ/(22\angle60^\circ) = 17.27\angle90^\circ = 0+j17.273\) A

\(\mathbf{I}_A = \mathbf{I}_{AB}-\mathbf{I}_{CA} = 14.957 - j8.636 = 17.27\angle-30.0^\circ\) A

Note \(17.27 \ne \sqrt3\times17.27\): the \(\sqrt3\) rule is only for balanced loads. Here the two phase currents happen to be equal in magnitude but 60° apart, so their difference has the same magnitude again.
